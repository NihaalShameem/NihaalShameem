Probability Fundamentals

Probability is the math of uncertainty — and every metric you live by (conversion, churn, win rate, fraud likelihood) is really a probability in disguise. This module builds the rules from scratch, then uses them to answer questions like "does requiring KYC actually change the odds a player deposits?"

🎯 What you'll be able to do

  • Define sample space, events, and probability precisely.
  • Estimate any probability straight from data as a relative frequency.
  • Apply the complement, addition, and multiplication rules.
  • Compute and interpret conditional probability P(A | B).
  • Test whether two events are independent.
  • Use Bayes' theorem to flip a conditional and update beliefs with evidence.

2.1  Sample space, events, probability

The sample space is the set of all possible outcomes. For "what happens to a new signup?" the space is {deposits, never deposits}. An event is any outcome (or set of outcomes) you care about — e.g. "the player deposits." A probability is a number from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain) measuring how likely an event is.

∑ Probability as relative frequency
P(event) = (number of times the event happens) / (total number of trials)
This is the bridge from a database to probability: a COUNT(...) / COUNT(*) is an estimated probability.

2.2  Probability from data: conversion rate

"What's the probability a registered player deposits?" — that's just the conversion rate. We define the event "converted" as made at least one deposit, then count.

SELECT COUNT(DISTINCT p.player_id) AS total_players, COUNT(DISTINCT d.player_id) AS depositors, ROUND(1.0 * COUNT(DISTINCT d.player_id) / COUNT(DISTINCT p.player_id), 4) AS p_convert FROM players p LEFT JOIN deposits d ON d.player_id = p.player_id; -- NOTE: a LEFT JOIN fans out one row per deposit, so we must count -- DISTINCT players, not COUNT(*) — a classic, costly SQL mistake.
💡 Watch the 1.0 *
SQL integer division would turn 329 / 500 into 0. Multiplying by 1.0 forces floating-point math. This bites every analyst once — now it won't be you.

In Python it's a boolean mean — one of the most useful one-liners you'll ever write, because True counts as 1 and False as 0.

depositor_ids = set(deposits["player_id"]) players["converted"] = players["player_id"].isin(depositor_ids) print("P(convert) =", round(players["converted"].mean(), 4)) # The complement rule: P(not convert) = 1 - P(convert) print("P(no deposit) =", round(1 - players["converted"].mean(), 4))

2.3  The three rules

RuleFormulaUse it when…
ComplementP(not A) = 1 − P(A)The "at least one" trick
Addition (OR)P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B)Either event can happen
Multiplication (AND)P(A and B) = P(A) · P(B | A)Both events happen together
🧠 Why subtract P(A and B)?
If you add the players who deposit and the players who bet on sportsbook, anyone who does both got counted twice. The addition rule subtracts that overlap once to fix the double count. (If A and B can't both happen — mutually exclusive — the overlap is 0 and it simplifies to P(A) + P(B).)
🎰 The "at least one" trick in practice
"What's the probability a sportsbook bettor wins at least one of their next 5 bets?" is painful to compute directly. The complement makes it easy: 1 − P(loses all 5) = 1 − (1 − p)⁵. You'll formalize this with the binomial distribution in Module 3.

2.4  Conditional probability

P(A | B) — "the probability of A given B" — restricts the world to cases where B happened, then asks how often A also happens. This is the single most important idea in applied analytics, because almost every business question is conditional: conversion given a channel, churn given a tier, win given the odds.

∑ Conditional probability
P(A | B) = P(A and B) / P(B)

Question: does identity verification (KYC) change the odds a player deposits? Compare P(convert | KYC verified) against P(convert | not verified).

SELECT p.kyc_verified, COUNT(DISTINCT p.player_id) AS players, COUNT(DISTINCT d.player_id) AS depositors, ROUND(1.0 * COUNT(DISTINCT d.player_id) / COUNT(DISTINCT p.player_id), 3) AS p_convert FROM players p LEFT JOIN deposits d ON d.player_id = p.player_id GROUP BY p.kyc_verified;
depositor_ids = set(deposits["player_id"]) players["converted"] = players["player_id"].isin(depositor_ids) by_channel = (players.groupby("acquisition_channel")["converted"] .agg(p_convert="mean", players="count") .round(3) .sort_values("p_convert", ascending=False)) print(by_channel)
💡 What this tells the business
Verified players convert at about 69% versus 47% for unverified — a 22-point gap. Whether KYC causes conversion or just correlates with serious players is a different question (Module 13, Causal Inference). But for forecasting and targeting, the conditional probability is gold.

2.5  Independence

Two events are independent if knowing one tells you nothing about the other. Formally:

∑ Independence
A ⊥ B ⇔ P(A | B) = P(A) ⇔ P(A and B) = P(A) · P(B)

Since P(convert | verified) = 0.69 ≠ P(convert) = 0.66, conversion and KYC status are not independent — they're related. Let's verify the multiplication test directly: if independent, the observed joint probability would equal the product of the marginals.

depositor_ids = set(deposits["player_id"]) players["converted"] = players["player_id"].isin(depositor_ids) p_convert = players["converted"].mean() p_verified = (players["kyc_verified"] == 1).mean() p_joint = ((players["converted"]) & (players["kyc_verified"] == 1)).mean() print(f"P(convert) = {p_convert:.3f}") print(f"P(verified) = {p_verified:.3f}") print(f"P(convert AND verified) observed = {p_joint:.3f}") print(f"...would be if independent = {p_convert * p_verified:.3f}") print("Observed > independent estimate => positively associated, NOT independent.")

2.6  Bayes' theorem

Bayes' theorem flips a conditional probability around. You often know P(evidence | hypothesis) but want P(hypothesis | evidence). It's the engine behind fraud scoring, churn models, and responsible-gaming flags.

∑ Bayes' theorem
P(H | E) = P(E | H) · P(H) / P(E) P(H) prior — belief before evidence P(E | H) likelihood — how expected the evidence is if H is true P(H | E) posterior — updated belief after seeing evidence
🎰 The question
A player just funded their wallet with crypto. How much should that raise our belief that they're a high-value player (VIP or Whale)? We know the pieces: the base rate of high-value players, and how often high-value players use crypto. Bayes turns those into the answer we actually want — P(high-value | crypto).
crypto_players = set(deposits.loc[deposits["method"] == "Crypto", "player_id"]) players["used_crypto"] = players["player_id"].isin(crypto_players) players["high_value"] = players["value_tier"].isin(["VIP", "Whale"]) # The ingredients prior = players["high_value"].mean() # P(H) likelihood = players.loc[players["high_value"], "used_crypto"].mean() # P(E|H) p_evidence = players["used_crypto"].mean() # P(E) posterior = likelihood * prior / p_evidence # Bayes direct = players.loc[players["used_crypto"], "high_value"].mean() # ground truth print(f"prior P(high-value) = {prior:.3f}") print(f"likelihood P(crypto|high-val) = {likelihood:.3f}") print(f"evidence P(crypto) = {p_evidence:.3f}") print(f"posterior P(high-val|crypto) = {posterior:.3f} (Bayes)") print(f"direct P(high-val|crypto) = {direct:.3f} (count-it-up check)")
💡 The payoff
The prior is ~12%; after learning the player used crypto, the posterior jumps to ~22% — crypto usage nearly doubles the odds they're high-value. That's Bayesian updating: evidence moves belief. And the count-it-up "direct" number matches the Bayes result exactly, proving the theorem isn't magic — it's just careful bookkeeping of conditional probabilities.

✏️ Exercise 2 — The complement / "at least one"
A sportsbook bettor wins any single bet with probability p ≈ 0.40 (the sportsbook win rate from Module 1's data). Using only the complement rule, what's the probability they win at least one of their next 5 bets? Assume bets are independent.
p = 0.40 n = 5 # P(at least one win) = 1 - P(zero wins)
Show solution
p, n = 0.40, 5 p_at_least_one = 1 - (1 - p) ** n print(f"P(>=1 win in {n} bets) = {p_at_least_one:.4f}") # ~0.92 — the complement turns a messy sum into one subtraction.
🧠 Key takeaways
  • A probability is a COUNT / COUNT — your database is full of them.
  • Conditional probability P(A|B) restricts the world to B — it's how every segmented metric works.
  • Independence means P(A|B) = P(A); if they differ, the events carry information about each other.
  • Bayes flips P(E|H) into P(H|E) — the foundation of scoring, fraud, and churn models.
