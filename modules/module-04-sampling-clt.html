Sampling & the Central Limit Theorem

This is the module that makes inference possible. You almost never measure the whole population — you measure a sample and reason about the rest. The Central Limit Theorem is the near-magical result that lets you do this reliably, and it's the hidden engine inside every confidence interval and A/B test you'll ever run.

🎯 What you'll be able to do

  • Explain why we sample and what makes a sample representative vs. biased.
  • Describe the sampling distribution of a statistic.
  • Compute the standard error and explain what it measures.
  • State and demonstrate the Central Limit Theorem on skewed iGaming data.
  • Draw random and stratified samples in SQL and Python.
  • Understand why bigger samples give more precise estimates — and by how much.

4.1  Why sample at all?

Sometimes the population is too big or too expensive to measure in full. More often in analytics, the population is partly in the future: when you estimate a new campaign's conversion rate from its first 300 signups, those 300 are a sample of all the signups that campaign will eventually produce. Sampling well is what lets a number from the past predict the future.

🎰 Bias is the silent killer
If you estimate "average deposit" only from players who opened the VIP email, you'll massively overestimate — that's selection bias. A representative sample looks like the population in miniature. Survivorship bias (only studying still-active players) and self-selection (only surveying engaged users) are the traps that quietly wreck analyses.

4.2  Drawing a sample

A simple random sample gives every row an equal chance. In SQL that's ORDER BY RANDOM(); in pandas it's .sample().

SELECT ROUND(AVG(amount), 2) AS sample_mean, COUNT(*) AS n FROM ( SELECT amount FROM deposits ORDER BY RANDOM() LIMIT 30 ); -- Re-run this a few times. The sample mean jumps around the true mean (~$73) -- every time. That jumpiness is exactly what the standard error measures.

Stratified sampling samples within each group so the mix matches the population — essential when a small group (whales) matters a lot. Here's the pandas pattern: take 10% from each value tier.

strat = players.groupby("value_tier").sample(frac=0.10, random_state=1) print("population tier mix:") print((players["value_tier"].value_counts(normalize=True) * 100).round(1)) print("\nstratified sample tier mix (matches population):") print((strat["value_tier"].value_counts(normalize=True) * 100).round(1))

4.3  The sampling distribution & standard error

Here's the key mental shift. A statistic like the sample mean x̄ is itself a random variable — take a different sample, get a different x̄. The distribution of x̄ over all possible samples is the sampling distribution, and its standard deviation has a special name: the standard error (SE).

∑ Standard error of the mean
SE = σ / √n
σ is the population standard deviation, n is the sample size. The SE shrinks as n grows — but only with the square root of n. To halve your uncertainty you need 4× the data.
🧠 Standard deviation vs. standard error
Standard deviation describes the spread of individual values (how much do deposits vary?). Standard error describes the spread of an estimate (how much would my average deposit move if I resampled?). Mixing these two up is one of the most common mistakes in analytics.

4.4  The Central Limit Theorem

Deposits are wildly skewed (Module 1 & 3) — nothing like a bell curve. So here's the astonishing part:

∑ Central Limit Theorem (CLT)
The distribution of the sample mean approaches a Normal distribution as n grows, no matter what shape the original data has — centered at the true mean μ, with standard deviation SE = σ/√n. In practice n ≈ 30 is often enough.

Let's prove it on our own skewed deposit data. We'll draw thousands of samples of size 30, take each one's mean, and look at the distribution of those means.

import numpy as np, matplotlib.pyplot as plt amt = deposits["amount"].values mu, sigma = amt.mean(), amt.std(ddof=0) n = 30 rng = np.random.default_rng(7) sample_means = np.array([rng.choice(amt, size=n, replace=True).mean() for _ in range(5000)]) SE_formula = sigma / np.sqrt(n) print(f"population mean μ = {mu:.2f}") print(f"mean of sample means = {sample_means.mean():.2f} (≈ μ)") print(f"SE formula σ/√n = {SE_formula:.2f}") print(f"std of sample means = {sample_means.std():.2f} (≈ SE formula)") fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10, 4)) axes[0].hist(amt[amt < 400], bins=40, color="#36bcf7", edgecolor="white") axes[0].set_title("Raw deposits — skewed") axes[1].hist(sample_means, bins=40, color="#3fb950", edgecolor="white") axes[1].axvline(mu, color="#f85149", lw=2) axes[1].set_title(f"Means of samples (n={n}) — Normal!") plt.show()
💡 Why this is the whole ballgame
The skewed pile on the left became a clean bell curve on the right just by averaging. That is why we can put a confidence interval around an average deposit, or run a t-test on revenue per user, even though the underlying data is ugly and skewed. Every inference technique in Part II rests on this one theorem.

4.5  Sample size and precision

Because SE = σ/√n, precision improves with the square root of sample size. Let's make that concrete — watch how the standard error falls as n climbs.

import numpy as np sigma = deposits["amount"].std(ddof=0) print(f"{'n':>7} {'SE = σ/√n':>12}") for n in [10, 30, 100, 400, 1600, 6400]: print(f"{n:>7} {sigma/np.sqrt(n):>12.2f}") print("\nNote: each 4× increase in n only halves the SE.")
🎰 The business takeaway
This is why A/B tests need real traffic and why "we ran it for a day on 50 users" is never enough. Doubling your test's precision costs four times the sample. Knowing the √n law keeps you from over-promising on tiny samples — and from wastefully over-collecting when you already have enough.

🧠 Key takeaways
  • We reason about populations from representative samples; bias breaks everything.
  • A statistic has its own distribution — the sampling distribution.
  • SE = σ/√n measures how much an estimate would wobble if you resampled.
  • The CLT: sample means go Normal regardless of the data's shape — the basis of all inference.
  • Precision grows with √n: 4× the data for 2× the precision.
